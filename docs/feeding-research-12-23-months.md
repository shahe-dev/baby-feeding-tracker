# Feeding research for 12–23 months

Evidence review for Baby Feeding Tracker · Reviewed September 2026

This review covers feeding during **12–23 completed months**, using only WHO 2023 and its commissioned evidence reports, LEAP and its follow-ups, Scandinavian/Nordic primary research and official recommendations, and New Zealand BLISS. Studies with an intervention started in infancy are identified as such; toddler follow-up does not turn them into trials of first starting the intervention after the first birthday.

The original emphasis on nutrient-dense meals has a sound foundation. WHO supports daily animal-source foods. BLISS supplies a useful meal structure, and LEAP supports a specific peanut-maintenance regimen after early introduction. Those findings have distinct boundaries. They do not make the original summary's entire schedule a tested toddler diet. [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240081864); [BLISS protocol, Daniels et al., 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12887-015-0491-8); [LEAP trial regimen](https://www.leapstudy.co.uk/leap-0.html).

| Evidence family | What it can contribute at this age | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| WHO 2023 | A framework covering 6-23 months, with explicit distinctions for 12-23 months | Public-health recommendations; many have low-certainty evidence |
| LEAP / LEAP-On / LEAP-Trio | Peanut prevention and persistence of protection after early consumption | Not a complete diet or a first-introduction trial at this age |
| Nordic guidance and trials | Age-specific composition, dietary variety and relevant toddler outcomes | Broad Nordic guidance and individual studies answer different questions |
| NZ BLISS | Supported self-feeding, meal composition and outcomes at 12/24 months | Most papers analyse one trial, with the intervention begun in infancy |

## Relationship to the original project

The [original research synthesis](revised-evidence-based-feeding-schedule.md) is preserved unchanged as an archive. It names the research families but does not include the complete selected documents or bibliography. NNR2023, OTIS and PreventADALL are further research within the permitted Nordic family, not documents confirmed to have been selected originally. Specific Scandinavian study matches and remaining uncertainty are stated below.

The original plan is dairy-free and discusses cow's-milk protein allergy. That is a project constraint, not a diagnosis made by the tracker. The app must preserve recorded restrictions and individual feeding instructions. Its recipes are authored examples, and neither this review nor a birthday transition establishes an appropriate milk replacement or a nutritionally complete individual diet.

## 1. Meal composition after the first birthday

### WHO 2023 supports nutrient density and variety

WHO recommends daily meat, fish or eggs and daily fruits and vegetables; pulses, nuts and seeds should appear frequently. It discourages starch-dominated complementary diets and prefers whole grains when grains are used. It does not specify meat at every meal, a meat-first serving order, a vegetable-to-fruit ratio, or a universal gram quota. Responsive feeding is recommended. These are generally strong recommendations supported by low-certainty evidence; strength and certainty describe different things. [WHO, 2023, Recommendations 4 and 7](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK596423/).

The tracker adaptation is to retain an animal-source-food check across the day and prompts for variety. A recipe should not receive an evidence badge merely because it contains meat, and a whole-grain or legume option should not automatically fail the plan.

### The age distinction changes the interpretation of starch

The WHO-commissioned Optifood analysis modelled separate breastfed and non-breastfed 12-23-month diets. Its best-case patterns could meet the selected target nutrients, although vitamin D remained a non-target gap. Eliminating animal-source protein foods introduced iron/B12 gaps in some scenarios. In breastfed toddlers, increasing staple quantities did not introduce new target-nutrient gaps under the model's other assumptions. This differs from the youngest infant scenarios, where food volume and nutrient density were more limiting.

These are computer-optimised diets with food-composition and absorption assumptions, not feeding trials. They support attention to displacement and the whole diet; they do not prove that grains must be absent or that any ordinary home menu meets requirements. [Arimond, Ferguson and Wiesmann, June 2022, *Insights from Optifood modeling*, sections 4.3.3-4.3.4, commissioned for WHO 2023](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/nutrition-and-food-safety/complementary-feeding/cf-guidelines/dietary-modelling.pdf?sfvrsn=8e2abbff_3).

### Offer food without turning quantities into a test

The commissioned responsive-feeding review included 26 randomised trials and 10,009 participants. Components commonly included responding to hunger/fullness, avoiding pressure and encouraging self-feeding. Some interventions improved self-fed mouthfuls and reduced refusals at 20-23 months. Effects varied by intervention, setting and outcome; this is not evidence that an app can diagnose appetite or guarantee adequate intake. [IECS, November 2021, *Safety and effectiveness of responsive feeding*, WHO evidence review](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/nutrition-and-food-safety/complementary-feeding/cf-guidelines/systematic-review-responsive-feeding.pdf?sfvrsn=2a46329d_3).

The reviewed sources do not establish the old schedule's fixed 1-2 oz of animal food at every meal as a universal requirement. For the app, meal slots and starting portions should remain editable planning choices, with offered and eaten amounts recorded separately. Exact meal frequency should not be falsely attributed to a recommendation that did not test it.

## 2. Peanut and other allergens

### What LEAP actually tested

LEAP randomised 640 infants with severe eczema, egg allergy or both, enrolled from at least four to under eleven months. Consumption continued until age five. At that endpoint, peanut allergy affected 13.7% of avoiders versus 1.9% of consumers in the initially skin-test-negative cohort; corresponding figures in the initially positive cohort were 35.3% versus 10.6%. These are trial-population results, not an individual prediction. [Du Toit et al., 2015, *Randomized Trial of Peanut Consumption*, NEJM 372:803-813](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/randomized-trial-of-peanut-consumption-in-infants-at-risk-for-pea/).

The consumption regimen was **6 g of peanut protein per week across at least three meals**, continued to age five. Peanut-protein grams are not peanut-butter grams. The toddler years were included in maintenance, but LEAP did not compare alternative doses to establish a minimum effective dose. Three exposure ticks alone cannot verify the amount used in the trial. [LEAP study team's protocol summary](https://www.leapstudy.co.uk/leap-0.html).

For a child already eating tolerated peanut, this is relevant research context. First introduction at 12-23 months was not the randomised question. LEAP also used screening and baseline challenges; its prevention protocol does not authorise home treatment of suspected or established peanut allergy.

### Follow-ups do not supply missed-dose rules

After approximately four years of early consumption, LEAP-On tested a year of avoidance. At age six, allergy remained lower in the original consumption group: 4.8% versus 18.6%. The authors state that the minimum prior consumption duration needed for stable tolerance remains unknown. This cannot establish when a toddler may safely stop, or what a missed week means. [Du Toit et al., 2016, *Effect of Avoidance on Peanut Allergy after Early Peanut Consumption*, NEJM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12333932/).

LEAP-Trio found allergy at the age-12 assessment in 4.4% of former consumers versus 15.4% of former avoiders. Outcomes were available for 497 of the original 640 participants. Later intake was family-selected, so durable protection does not determine an optimal toddler restart or catch-up schedule. [Du Toit et al., 2024, *Follow-up to Adolescence*, NEJM Evidence](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38804779/).

### Other allergens need their own evidence

A LEAP cohort analysis found that protection was peanut-specific; it did not prevent other allergic disease or accelerate egg-allergy resolution. It cannot establish egg, sesame, fish, tree-nut or milk quotas. [Du Toit et al., 2018, *Allergen specificity of early peanut consumption*, JACI](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/allergen-specificity-of-early-peanut-consumption-and-effect-on-de/).

Newly identified within the permitted Scandinavian family, PreventADALL tested early peanut, milk, wheat and egg tastes in Norway/Sweden. Its infant-start intervention reduced food allergy at 36 months, mainly peanut allergy. It did not establish three-times-weekly maintenance at 12-23 months, and fish, sesame and tree nuts were not intervention foods. [Skjerven et al., 2022, *PreventADALL*, Lancet 399:2398-2411](https://www.oslo-universitetssykehus.no/48fd25/contentassets/eef1b42a88ae4fbba31d92ff6bf498b6/artikler-pdf/2022-early-food-intervention-and-skin-emollients.pdf).

## 3. BLISS outcomes at 12 and 24 months

### A practical meal structure, with measured limits

BLISS paired supported self-feeding with an iron-rich component, an energy-rich component and an easy-to-eat fruit or vegetable at meals. Its pilot recipes included red meat, liver, iron-fortified infant cereal and legumes as iron options. The pilot involved 23 self-selecting families, so it describes feasibility and design, not definitive superiority. [Cameron et al., 2015, *Development and pilot testing of BLISS*, BMC Pediatrics 15:99](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12887-015-0422-8).

The main trial recruited 206 families in Dunedin. Support began before complementary feeding, with follow-up at 12 and 24 months. The studies below largely analyse that same trial; they should not be counted as independent replications.

| Outcome | What was observed | What the app must not claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iron at 12 months | No significant between-group differences in iron intake or status; 83% of each group were iron sufficient | That BLISS prevented deficiency, or an iron-food checkbox proves adequacy |
| Growth at 12/24 months | No significant BMI z-score or energy-intake differences | Proven obesity prevention or superior growth |
| Diet at 24 months | Broadly similar nutrient intake; high sodium and added-sugar intakes remained common | That family foods automatically create an ideal diet |
| Variety at 24 months | About two additional distinct fruit/vegetable foods across three recording days in BLISS | Proven lifelong preference or developmental benefit |

The iron analysis had blood samples from 119 children, fewer than planned. Iron-deficiency anaemia was present in both groups. The result is compatible with supported self-feeding, but does not remove the need to assess inadequate intake or growth. [Daniels et al., 2018, *Impact of a modified version of baby-led weaning on iron intake and status*, BMJ Open 8:e019036](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/8/6/e019036).

The growth study had 166 participants available at 24 months. Parents reported less fussiness and more enjoyment at 12 months, but the findings did not demonstrate better energy self-regulation. [Taylor et al., 2017, *Effect of a Baby-Led Approach on Infant Growth and Overweight*, JAMA Pediatrics](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28692728/).

At 24 months, 68% exceeded the nutrient paper's sodium criterion and 75% its added-sugar criterion. Variety analyses also had substantial loss of dietary records at that age. [Erickson et al., 2018, *Food and Nutrient Intake*, Nutrients 10:740](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/10/6/740); [Morison et al., 2018, *Food Variety and Food Preferences*, Nutrients 10:1092](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30111722/).

### Choking and texture need careful wording

Choking was reported in the trial; no significant group difference was detected. The observations ran through 12 months and do not provide a toddler choking-rate estimate. Supported BLISS included explicit preparation teaching, upright seating and supervision. Its result cannot be described as zero risk or transferred to any unmodified self-feeding approach. [Fangupo et al., 2016, *A Baby-Led Approach to Eating Solids and Risk of Choking*, Pediatrics 138:e20160772](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27647715/).

Developmentally manageable textures and supported self-feeding belong in the tracker. A fixed shape rule from an infant protocol should not replace assessment of a toddler's current skills. [Daniels et al., 2015, BLISS protocol](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12887-015-0491-8).

## 4. Scandinavian evidence and the whole diet

### Age-specific Nordic reference values

NNR2023 gives the following ranges for **12-23 months**, as percentages of total dietary energy, including milk feeds: **protein 10-15%, fat 30-40%, carbohydrate 45-60%**. These guide dietary planning; they are not quotas that must be met at each meal. Fat quality matters, and the saturated-fat recommendation applies from 12 months. This supports adequate fat without the original unlimited-fat wording. [Nordic Council of Ministers, NNR2023, Boxes 3-4](https://pub.norden.org/nord2023-003/recommendations.html).

| Nutrient | NNR2023 recommended intake, single-year '1 y' row |
| --- | --- |
| Iron | 7 mg/day |
| Vitamin D | 10 micrograms/day |
| Calcium | 400 mg/day |
| Zinc | 4.0 mg/day |

These concern usual total intake and are not supplement prescriptions. The broader 1-3-year table uses different calcium/zinc values; the age band must be shown with any number. A day's diary cannot diagnose a deficiency. [NNR2023, Appendix 6](https://pub.norden.org/nord2023-003/appendix.html).

### Directly relevant newer Swedish trial

OTIS is newly identified research within the allowed family. It randomised 250 healthy term infants to a supported, protein-reduced Nordic complementary diet or conventional Swedish recommendations; 206 completed follow-up. At 12 and 18 months, the Nordic group ate 42-45% more fruit/vegetables and less protein, with no detected differences in growth, body composition, energy intake or iron status.

This supports the feasibility of that designed intervention during the relevant age window. It does not prove that an improvised plant-heavy or dairy-free menu has the same composition or adequacy, or establish long-term superiority. It does challenge the idea that more animal protein is necessarily the better-supported approach. [Johansson et al., 2023, *A Nordic, protein-reduced complementary diet*, American Journal of Clinical Nutrition 117:1219-1231](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/13465d77-143f-491a-8863-ebeda9d24206).

### What the original fish references can establish

The strongest ABIS match is Klingberg and colleagues' Swedish cohort of 9,727 children. Earlier fish introduction was associated with lower subsequent asthma odds. It was observational, did not distinguish fish species and did not test a toddler fish dose. [Klingberg et al., 2019, *Introduction of fish and other foods during infancy and risk of asthma*, European Journal of Pediatrics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00431-018-03312-5).

The likely eczema reference is a different Western Sweden cohort, not ABIS. Fish introduction before nine months was associated with lower reported eczema at one year (adjusted odds ratio 0.76; 95% CI 0.62-0.94). Confounding and reverse causation limit prevention claims. Neither paper establishes a twice-weekly toddler threshold or treatment of existing eczema. [Alm et al., 2009, *Early introduction of fish decreases the risk of eczema in infants*, Archives of Disease in Childhood](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18818269/).

## 5. The dairy-free constraint and individual quantities

### The selected evidence does not choose a milk replacement

WHO 2023's general milk recommendation for non-breastfed 12-23-month-olds is conditional and low-certainty. It does not establish a cow's-milk-allergy treatment plan. WHO found insufficient evidence to recommend animal versus plant milk, or full-fat versus lower-fat milk, for this age. [WHO 2023, Recommendation 2](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK596430/).

The commissioned milk review largely concerns apparently healthy children. Its animal-versus-plant comparison rested on a single 21-participant trial measuring blood lipids, with very-low-certainty evidence. That is insufficient to certify a particular plant drink as a nutritionally adequate replacement for a child with milk allergy. [Fernandez-Gaxiola, De-Regil and Gallegos-Lecona, *Animal milks compared to follow-on formula, low-fat milk, plant-based milk or fortified milk*, evidence report linked to WHO 2023](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/nutrition-and-food-safety/complementary-feeding/cf-guidelines/systematic-review-milks-12-23-months.pdf?sfvrsn=f968c9f2_3).

The practical consequence is to retain the existing restriction and any prescribed feeds. Before personalising the menu, a paediatric clinician or dietitian needs the actual milk/formula product and intake, current allergy advice, growth and typical food quantities. The selected corpus does not justify changing those choices automatically at the first birthday.

### How to turn the research into meal planning

The following is an authored implementation framework, not a published weekly menu or an individually calculated diet:

| Planning question | Proposed behaviour |
| --- | --- |
| Is an iron-rich component available? | Identify it within the meal; do not equate a food name with iron adequacy |
| Is the meal sufficiently energy-dense? | Show ingredients contributing energy; avoid a vegetable-only meal default |
| Are vegetables and fruit represented? | Record variety over time; do not invent an evidence-based ratio |
| Are animal-source foods represented through the day? | Retain a daily planning check, without forcing meat into every meal |
| Is an allergen already tolerated? | Plan continued inclusion under the existing individual plan; keep reactions and restrictions visible |
| What did the child actually consume? | Separate offered, eaten, refused and unknown; retain quantities and context |

This combines the findings in sections 1-4. Exact recipes, portion defaults and a three-meal/two-snack screen layout would be authored conveniences. Label these choices as practical adaptations; none is a published WHO, LEAP or BLISS schedule.

### Questions that remain individual

A personalised calorie target, grams of each food, milk replacement, supplement doses and first introduction of a previously uneaten allergen cannot be determined from age and these papers alone. The appropriate response to that gap is to keep the app's defaults flexible and store any existing individual instructions accurately.

## 6. Correcting the original claims and app

The original source document should remain accessible, with a separate evidence review. A source-preserving correction should not reinstate every assertion as current medical guidance.

| Original claim or feature | Finding and correction |
| --- | --- |
| All research converges on the listed principles | Different studies answer different questions. Show the study, population and scope beside each rule. |
| Every allergen at least three times weekly indefinitely | LEAP supplies a peanut regimen. The reviewed Nordic trial does not validate the universal quota. |
| Weekly checklist prioritises any allergen below three exposures | LEAP follow-ups supply no toddler missed-dose or catch-up rule. Record actual intake without translating ticks into allergy risk. |
| BLISS prevented iron deficiency | The trial found no significant iron-status difference; deficiency still occurred. |
| Each meal must include 1-2 oz of iron-rich animal food | BLISS iron options also included legumes and fortified cereal. The studies do not validate that universal portion rule. |
| Fat is unrestricted below two | Use the age-specific Nordic range and food-quality guidance. |
| Vegetables should outnumber fruit; grains are not foundational | The studies do not establish a vegetable-to-fruit ratio. WHO cautions against starch-dominated diets; assess nutrient density across the whole diet. |
| ABIS early fish introduction reduced eczema and asthma | The asthma finding is observational; the likely eczema reference is a separate cohort. Neither establishes a toddler fish-frequency threshold. |
| Prepared baby-led feeding has no increased choking risk and improves oral motor development | BLISS detected no group difference in choking through 12 months. It did not establish superior oral motor development. |
| Dairy-free recipes presented as a complete plan | Milk replacement and the complete diet remain unassessed. |

The supporting studies and links for each judgement appear in the preceding sections. This audit also leaves the original summary's precise brain-growth, absorption-rate and commercial-food-failure statistics unverified; they should not drive app rules without identifiable evidence in the permitted source set.

### Implementation requirements

Keep the storage, recovery, editing, offline and ingredient-recording improvements. Keep access to the unchanged original research summary and a traceable evidence register for current guidance. Distinguish original source families from newly found Nordic studies.

For each feeding rule, identify the source, age/population, evidence limits and whether it is a study protocol, guideline recommendation or a practical adaptation. Keep animal-food and meal-composition prompts, accurate peanut research context, individual allergen restrictions and observed intake. A personal rotation target can be recorded as a planning preference; it must not become a claimed prevention threshold for every allergen.

Preserve date-based age calculation. Apply these research distinctions to the toddler menu and retain the diary when the child is outside the reviewed age range. Individual plans and restrictions must remain editable.

## 7. Evidence quality and research limits

### What was reviewed

This was a targeted primary-source review of the four permitted research families, including WHO's commissioned modelling and systematic reviews. It examined meal composition, energy and protein, iron, allergens, feeding skills, dietary variety and the dairy-free gap. Searches and full-source follow-ups stopped when each consequential question had either relevant evidence or an explicit limitation. This is not a registered systematic review of all toddler nutrition research.

The sources range from public-health guidance and computer modelling to randomised trials and observational cohorts. A guideline's strong recommendation is not synonymous with high-certainty trial evidence. Follow-up publications from LEAP and outcome papers from BLISS are not separate replications.

### Source identification and research provenance

The original synthesis did not provide a complete bibliography. WHO 2023 and LEAP are identifiable; BLISS has a defined publication series. The ABIS asthma paper is the strongest match for the original Swedish asthma claim. The Western Sweden eczema paper is a likely match for a different outcome and must not be attributed to ABIS.

**NNR2023, OTIS and PreventADALL were newly identified within the permitted Scandinavian/Nordic family.** They are included as further research, not as documents proven to have been selected for the original project. The 2024 Nordic infant-feeding scoping review mainly covers 0-12 months and was used to identify relevant publications; it is not treated as a comprehensive toddler guideline. [Hornell and Lagstrom, 2024, *Infant feeding*, Food & Nutrition Research 68:10456](https://foodandnutritionresearch.net/index.php/fnr/article/view/10456).

PubMed, institutional repositories and trial websites host the named research; they are not additional guidance authorities. The evidence base is restricted to the four stated research families. This review does not add CDC, NHS or commercial parenting guidance.

### Individual application

Age alone cannot establish calorie needs, a suitable milk replacement, supplement doses, current allergen tolerance or nutritional adequacy. Clinical instructions, actual milk/formula intake, growth and representative food quantities are needed to assess an individual diet. Food records and flexible meal examples can support that assessment; they cannot certify it.

The [original synthesis remains preserved unchanged](revised-evidence-based-feeding-schedule.md), with its [archive status explained separately](README.md). Its precise brain-growth, absorption-rate and commercial-food-failure statistics remain unverified within this source set and must not drive app rules.
